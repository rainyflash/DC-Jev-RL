\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What sharing the backbone buys.} The judge on the shared backbone gives the same answers as the judge
loaded on its own, and the trainer holds one copy of the backbone instead of two: \NUM{mem_saving_load} less GPU memory
after loading. For a 4B policy on a 96\,GB GPU this is a convenience; on a smaller GPU, or with a larger model, it can
decide whether a judge fits next to the policy at all. It costs \NUM{adapter_switch_ms}\,ms per adapter switch and
some speed from applying the judge's adapter unmerged.

\paragraph{What the judge adds on top of the verifier.} One seed of \texttt{rlvr\_kev\_add} and one of
\texttt{rlvr\_kev\_gate} end far above every \texttt{rlvr} seed, and they lift their conditions' means above
\texttt{rlvr}'s. These two runs differ from the others in that they switched to drafting first, which
\NUM{alldraftruns} runs of this project did in all, \NUM{allveronlydraft} of them with the verifier as their only reward; the other seeds
of the same conditions end near \texttt{rlvr}. With 2--3 seeds per condition we cannot tell whether the judge in the
reward makes such a switch more likely (here 2 of the 5 runs with \kev{}'s score added or as a gate, and none of the 3
\texttt{rlvr} runs). Where the judge can act at all is narrow. The verifier gives every completion an exact verdict,
and with $\lambda<1$ the judge can only reorder completions with the same verdict: among wrong completions, by preferring
the ones closer to right, and in groups where every completion is wrong, where the verifier gives no signal at all.
After the first steps few groups are all wrong (\NUM{allwrong_rlvr} over the last 20 steps of \texttt{rlvr}), and among
same-verdict completions a judge's preference can be about style as much as about correctness: \kev{} pays for solving
the problem again after the answer (\S\ref{sec:res-repeat}), and \NUM{allkevrepeat} of the \NUM{allkevruns} runs of this
project with \kev{}'s score in the reward ended doing so, against \NUM{allnokevrepeat} of the \NUM{allnokevruns} others.
None of these repeating runs became more accurate for it.

\paragraph{Seeds and styles.} The largest differences between our runs are not between reward conditions but between
the styles the final policies write in. The runs that switched to drafting first reach \NUM{alldraftaime24min} to
\NUM{alldraftaime24max}\% on AIME24, every other run of the project at most \NUM{allotheraime24max}\%. The switch was
abrupt: in each of these runs the share of drafts among the MATH-500 completions rose from at most
\NUM{alldraftjumpbeforemax} to at least \NUM{alldraftjumpaftermin} between two evaluations 50 steps apart, starting at
step \NUM{alldraftjumpfrommin} at the earliest and step \NUM{alldraftjumpfrommax} at the latest. The verifier's verdicts
on the training completions do not show what set it off. A comparison of reward variants
with one to three seeds per condition, as ours, can mistake such a switch for an effect of the reward. We therefore
report every run and its style, and a comparison of RLVR variants on a small model should do the same, or use enough
seeds to estimate how often each variant switches. Whether drafting causes the higher accuracy or only comes with it,
we did not test; forcing or forbidding the draft opening at evaluation, or in training, would be a first test.

\paragraph{A judge is not a verifier.} Offline, \kev{}'s $\pc$ separates correct from incorrect base-policy solutions
with an AUROC near 0.9 (\S\ref{sec:res-study}). The probes showed that it can be moved by edits that do not change the
answer, if only slightly (a claim that every step was verified). Trained against $\pc$ alone, the policy found
solutions the judge overrates: its wrong answers reach a $\pc$ the judge used to give only to right ones, the judge's
ranking within a group decays towards chance, and the accuracy on every benchmark but GSM8K falls below the base
model's. This is the reward over-optimisation that \citet{gao2023scaling} describe, at small scale and within 200
steps. An offline AUROC measures a judge on the distribution it was trained for, not on the distribution a policy
optimised against it will produce, nor on the styles a policy may drift into on its own.

\paragraph{Limitations.} One policy and judge size (4B), one task (MATH, evaluated on MATH-500, GSM8K and three
competition sets), 200 steps, one learning rate chosen for \texttt{rlvr}, one judge weight, and 2--3 seeds per
condition, too few to say how often a condition ends in each style. The intervals resample problems and not seeds.
Greedy evaluation on vLLM varies by about \NUM{szsd_math500} points between repeated evaluations of the same model
(\S\ref{sec:res-cond}). The two style measures were defined after we had read the final completions. The policies'
weights are not in the repository (their completions, logs and training completions are), so interventions on the
final policies cannot be run from it. Other experiments shared the GPUs during training, so we report no training
times except those measured on an idle GPU.

\paragraph{Relation to the earlier versions.} Versions 1--3 of this paper reported a leaderboard on which \kev{}'s
reward improved on the verifier, and memory savings computed from an assumed factor. Neither was measured (see the
notice at the top and \file{CORRECTION.md}). The measured answer: the shared backbone saves one copy of the backbone
and leaves the judge unchanged, and adding \kev{}'s judgment to the verifier did not reliably improve GRPO on MATH in
our runs. Two of its runs did improve a lot, together with a change of writing style that runs without the judge also
made.
