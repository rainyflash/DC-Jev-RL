\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Within a problem, the judge sees little of where a solution is going.} Offline, \kev{}'s \emph{on track}
score separates the correct from the incorrect continuations of one problem's 128-token prefixes with an AUROC of
\NUM{aucw_128_kevtrack}, no better than the AR judge of the same size (\NUM{aucw_128_artrack}) or the policy's own
log-probability (\NUM{aucw_128_logprob}), which costs nothing. Across problems every signal does better, because each
partly sees how hard the problem is, and that does not help to choose among one problem's prefixes. In training, on
the policy's own roots, the separation was weaker still (\NUM{auditotaucw_all_treekev}
\ci{\NUM{auditotaucwlo_all_treekev}}{\NUM{auditotaucwhi_all_treekev}}), and keeping \kev{}'s two roots of four raised
the share of correct leaves by \NUM{auditgain_all_treekev}
\ci{\NUM{auditgainlo_all_treekev}}{\NUM{auditgainhi_all_treekev}} points, against \NUM{auditgain_all_treerand} for a
random choice and \NUM{auditbest_all_treekev} for the best two in hindsight. The gain was larger early in training
(\NUM{auditgain_firsthalf_treekev} points in the first half, \NUM{auditgain_secondhalf_treekev} in the second, without
intervals), while the policy's roots came to lead to correct answers more often and the room for a choice shrank (best
two in hindsight: \NUM{auditbest_firsthalf_treekev} and \NUM{auditbest_secondhalf_treekev} points).

\paragraph{A small gain at the roots did not change what the policy learned.} With the same tree shape, keeping
\kev{}'s roots instead of random ones left the final accuracy unchanged within the intervals over problems on all
five benchmarks; on GSM8K the difference, \NUM{dvtreerand_treekev_gsm}
\ci{\NUM{dvtreerandlo_treekev_gsm}}{\NUM{dvtreerandhi_treekev_gsm}} points, has an interval that just reaches zero. The
idea behind the judge's choice is that it gives GRPO more groups with mixed outcomes, the only groups with a
learning signal. It did not: in the last 20 steps \NUM{allwrong_treekev} of the groups had no correct leaf under
\texttt{tree\_kev} and \NUM{allwrong_treerand} under \texttt{tree\_random}, and \NUM{allright_treekev} and
\NUM{allright_treerand} had only correct ones. The choice has a cost in principle: the kept roots are no longer samples
of the policy, so the gradient is no longer an unbiased estimate of the policy gradient. We saw no effect of that on
accuracy. Nor did the judge's choice make the policy repeat itself: one seed of \texttt{tree\_kev} came to repeat its
solution after the first answer, but so did one seed of \texttt{rlvr}, which has no judge at all, and \kev{} scores only
the first 128 tokens of a solution, long before the answer.

\paragraph{The tree shape: the seeds that switched decide the means.} Both tree conditions end above flat sampling in
the seed mean, by \NUM{d_treerand_math} and \NUM{d_treekev_math} points on MATH-500 and \NUM{d_treerand_aime24} and
\NUM{d_treekev_aime24} on AIME24, but most of that comes from the \NUM{treedraftruns} tree runs that switched to
opening their solutions in plain text. These end at \NUM{treedraftaime24min}--\NUM{treedraftaime24max}\% on AIME24,
the others at \NUM{treeotheraime24min}--\NUM{treeotheraime24max}\% and \texttt{rlvr} at
\NUM{flataime24min}--\NUM{flataime24max}\%. The same switch marks the best runs of the whole project, whatever their
reward (Part II, \S8.3). Here \NUM{treedraftruns} of the tree runs made it and none of \texttt{rlvr}'s
\NUM{flatruns} seeds. Whether trees make it more likely, so few runs cannot tell, the less so as we did not plan for
the switch, found it after the runs had finished, and classify the runs by a measure defined after reading their
completions. In a tree the root settles how a completion opens, so the choice of roots is also a choice among
openings; a random choice of roots was enough for a run to switch. What the tree shape does buy for certain is tokens:
the trees generated \NUM{treegensavemin}--\NUM{treegensavemax} fewer tokens per step than flat sampling of completions
of the same lengths would have.

\paragraph{Limitations.} One policy size (4B), one judge question (\emph{on track}) asked of 128-token roots, one tree
shape (four roots, two kept, four leaves each), 200 steps at the learning rate of Part II, and \NUM{seeds_treekev}
seeds per tree condition (\texttt{rlvr}: \NUM{seeds_rlvr}), the third of each added after the first two had finished.
With more roots per prompt a judge has more to choose from, and so do its errors. The audit continues all roots only
every tenth step, 20 audited steps per run; its intervals resample those steps, not seeds. The intervals of the final
accuracies resample problems, not seeds. The offline study used the base policy's prefixes, not the training policy's.
The style measures were defined after reading the completions. We report tokens, not time: the runs shared GPUs with
other experiments.

\paragraph{Relation to the earlier versions.} Versions 1--3 of this paper reported pruning speed-ups, KV-cache reuse
rates, per-node latencies and theorem-proving results (MiniF2F-Lean4, ProofNet) that were not measured (see the notice
at the start and \file{CORRECTION.md}). None of these is measured here: we count tokens, not time, and ran no theorem
proving. What we could test, we did. The pruning rule of v3, prune a prefix when the judge's $p(\text{yes})<0.3$,
prunes \NUM{thrpruned_128_kevtrack} of the 128-token prefixes with the \emph{on track} question and catches
\NUM{thrcaught_128_kevtrack} of the dead ones (\S\ref{sec:res-prefix}). Choosing which roots to continue by \kev{}, the
idea behind those versions, did not make GRPO learn better than a random choice of roots.
