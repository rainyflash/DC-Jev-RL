\paragraph{The frozen judge makes things worse.} Rewarding the unlabelled completions with \kev{}'s $\pc$ as released
(\texttt{sub\_kev}) ends below dropping them, in both seeds (\NUM{acc_subkev_math_s1} and \NUM{acc_subkev_math_s2}\% on
MATH-500): \NUM{d_subkev_math} \ci{\NUM{dlo_subkev_math}}{\NUM{dhi_subkev_math}} points on MATH-500, \NUM{d_subkev_amc}
\ci{\NUM{dlo_subkev_amc}}{\NUM{dhi_subkev_amc}} on AMC23 and \NUM{d_subkev_aime24}
\ci{\NUM{dlo_subkev_aime24}}{\NUM{dhi_subkev_aime24}} on AIME24. Yet the judge kept ranking the unlabelled completions
well: its AUROC for the withheld labels was \NUM{fillauc_all_subkev} over the run (Table~\ref{tab:sub-fill}). It was
too generous, though. Its mean $\pc$ on the unlabelled completions was \NUM{fillmean_first20_subkev} in the first 20
steps, when \NUM{unlacc_first20_subkev}\% of them were correct, and \NUM{fillmean_last20_subkev} in the last 20, when
\NUM{unlacc_last20_subkev}\% were. Which completions are labelled is random, so a constant offset raises the expected
reward of every behaviour by the same amount, which the group baseline removes in expectation. A judge that pays more
for some behaviour than its correctness warrants changes what the policy learns. Both policies came to repeat
themselves: \NUM{srep_subkev_s1} and \NUM{srep_subkev_s2} of the lines of their MATH-500 solutions repeat an earlier line,
against \NUM{srep_sub_s1} and \NUM{srep_sub_s2} for \texttt{rlvr\_sub}, and \NUM{strunc_subkev_s1} of seed 1's solutions run into the
2048-token limit (Table~\ref{tab:sub-style}). After the first answer seed 1 restates its conclusion again and again,
often until the length limit, and seed 2 solves the problem again. Neither changes the answer the verifier reads, so the
labelled completions do not punish it, and on the unlabelled ones the judge pays for it (\S\ref{sec:res-repeat}).

\subsection{Drafting first}\label{sec:res-style}

Part II found that some GRPO runs switch, abruptly, to a style in which the completion opens with an informal draft in
plain text, without LaTeX or markdown, gives the answer, and often then writes the formatted solution; and that the runs that
switch are the best ones. We measure the style by the share of completions that open with such a draft (no math
delimiter, markdown heading or bold in the first 300 characters; Part II, \S8.3, defined after reading the
completions). The base model opens \NUM{pdraftat_0_min} to \NUM{pdraftat_0_max} of its MATH-500 completions this way.

\paragraph{Only the probe's policies switched.} Every run but one cut the share of drafts in its first 50 steps
(Table~\ref{tab:sub-drafts}). The exception is \texttt{sub\_probe} seed 1, which drafted in
\NUM{draftat_subprobe_s1_50} of its MATH-500 completions at step 50 and in \NUM{draftat_subprobe_s1_100} at step 100.
In seed 2 of \texttt{sub\_probe} the share fell to \NUM{draftat_subprobe_s2_50} at step 50 and then rose, from
\NUM{draftat_subprobe_s2_100} at step 100 to \NUM{draftat_subprobe_s2_150} at step 150. In the six other runs of the
label-budget conditions, and in the three of \texttt{rlvr}, it was at most \NUM{pdraftmax_other} in the final
evaluation. Across the \NUM{allruns} GRPO runs of this project, the \NUM{alldraftruns} that switched, in
\NUM{alldraftconds} different conditions, are the \NUM{alldraftruns} best on AIME24 (\NUM{alldraftaime24min} to \NUM{alldraftaime24max}\%, every other
run at most \NUM{allotheraime24max}\%; Part II, \S8.3). The gain of \texttt{sub\_probe} over \texttt{rlvr\_sub} comes
with this switch, and with two seeds we cannot separate the effect of the probe from that of the switch.

\paragraph{What the fill-in rewards paid for drafts.} Within a group, GRPO favours the completions with the higher
reward, so a style spreads when its completions are paid more than the others of their group. Table~\ref{tab:sub-draftgroups}
compares the drafts with the other completions of the same group by the verifier's verdict, and Table~\ref{tab:sub-draftfill}
by the fill-in reward among unlabelled completions with the same verdict. In steps 0--49 drafts were less often right
than the other completions of their group in all \NUM{pdgn_0} runs (between \NUM{pdgverdict_0_min} and
\NUM{pdgverdict_0_max} points), which is why their share fell. In both \texttt{sub\_probe} runs, from step 50 on, drafts
were as often right as the others or more (\NUM{dgverdict_subprobe_s1_50} and \NUM{dgverdict_subprobe_s1_100} points in
seed 1, \NUM{dgverdict_subprobe_s2_50} and \NUM{dgverdict_subprobe_s2_100} in seed 2), and in the windows in which
drafts spread the probe paid right drafts more than the other right completions (\NUM{dgfillright_subprobe_s1_50}
points in seed 1 over steps 50--99, \NUM{dgfillright_subprobe_s2_50} and \NUM{dgfillright_subprobe_s2_100} in seed 2
over steps 50--149). In the other fill-in runs the picture is mixed. In \texttt{sub\_kev\_recal} seed 2, drafts were
more often right than the others in the second half of the run (\NUM{dgverdict_subrecal_s2_100} and
\NUM{dgverdict_subrecal_s2_150} points), the recalibrated head paid right drafts less (\NUM{dgfillright_subrecal_s2_100}
and \NUM{dgfillright_subrecal_s2_150}), and drafts did not spread. In \texttt{sub\_kev\_recal} seed 1 the head paid right
drafts more over steps 100--199 (\NUM{dgfillright_subrecal_s1_100} and \NUM{dgfillright_subrecal_s1_150}), and drafts
did not spread either. A few windows of a few runs, read after the fact, do not tell what made the probe's runs switch,
and in Part III-C runs switched with the verifier's reward alone (Part II, \S8.3).

\subsection{Does the judge pay for repetition?}\label{sec:res-repeat}

After the GRPO runs we tested whether \kev{} pays for what the policies of \texttt{sub\_kev} and
\texttt{sub\_kev\_recal} write after their first answer, with the probe of Part II (\file{dcjev/llm/repeat_probe.py};
Table~\ref{tab:repeat-probe}). \texttt{sub\_kev}'s seed 1 restates its conclusion again and again;
\texttt{sub\_kev\_recal}'s seed 1 solves the problem again in nearly the same words, like the repeating policies of
Part II. We cut the final MATH-500 solutions of seed 1 that repeat lines after their first answer, as if the policy
had stopped there. Solutions that repeat before their first answer, or whose first answer differs from their last,
were left out (\NUM{rpskip_subkev} and \NUM{rpskip_subrecal}). No edit changes the answer the verifier reads.

The part after the first answer raises the released \kev{}'s $\pc$, \texttt{sub\_kev}'s fill-in reward, by
\NUM{rpkevc_cutsubkev_1} \ci{\NUM{rpkevclo_cutsubkev_1}}{\NUM{rpkevchi_cutsubkev_1}} on right answers and by
\NUM{rpkevc_cutsubkev_0} \ci{\NUM{rpkevclo_cutsubkev_0}}{\NUM{rpkevchi_cutsubkev_0}} on wrong ones. On
\texttt{sub\_kev\_recal}'s solutions it raises it by \NUM{rpkevc_cutsubrecal_1}
\ci{\NUM{rpkevclo_cutsubrecal_1}}{\NUM{rpkevchi_cutsubrecal_1}} and \NUM{rpkevc_cutsubrecal_0}
\ci{\NUM{rpkevclo_cutsubrecal_0}}{\NUM{rpkevchi_cutsubrecal_0}}, for \NUM{rpkevcup_cutsubrecal_0} of the wrong ones.
Repetition as such is worth little: written into the base policy's solutions, it raises \kev{}'s $\pc$ by
\NUM{rpkevc_end3_1} on right answers and changes it by \NUM{rpkevc_end3_0} on wrong ones. So on the unlabelled
completions the fill-in reward paid for what these policies learned to write after their first answer, most of all on
wrong answers, to which the verifier would have given nothing. The probe scores with the released head. The head of
\texttt{sub\_kev\_recal} was refitted during training, and its own preference at the end of the run is not measured
here. The AR judge, which no condition used, rates \texttt{sub\_kev\_recal}'s solutions higher with the part after the
first answer (\NUM{rparc_cutsubrecal_1} on right answers) but \texttt{sub\_kev}'s endless restatements lower
(\NUM{rparc_cutsubkev_1} on right answers, \NUM{rparc_cutsubkev_0} on wrong ones).
